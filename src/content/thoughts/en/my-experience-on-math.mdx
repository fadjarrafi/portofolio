## The Monster Under My Bed

Math was the monster under my bed for most of my school life. Not the kind you grow out of by fifth grade — the kind that followed me from elementary school all the way through senior year, sitting at the back of every classroom, waiting.

I remember one class in particular. We were deep into systems of linear equations: two variables, substitution, elimination, the whole drill. My teacher had just handed out a worksheet when one of my friends leaned over and muttered, half to me, half to the universe: "When are we ever going to use this in real life?"

I didn't have an answer. Worse, I was thinking the exact same thing.

## Choosing Computer Science Anyway

That question followed me longer than I expected. When it came time to pick a university major, I chose computer science, not because I'd made peace with math, but because I loved computers. I still remember getting my first PC in fifth grade, a Pentium III, and losing entire afternoons to it. Computer science felt like the natural next step for a kid who wanted to keep playing.

What I didn't understand yet was that "computer science" and "math" were basically roommates.

## Math's Revenge in College

The first semester made that painfully clear. Calculus, linear algebra, matrix theory, statistics, discrete mathematics. One after another, like the universe had decided to make me answer for every math class I'd resented in high school. There were weeks I sat in lectures feeling like my head was going to split open trying to hold all of it at once.

And then, in a twist I still find funny, I chose deep learning as my thesis topic — a field that runs almost entirely on the math I'd spent a decade avoiding. It was, without question, the hardest and most exhausting stretch of my academic life.

But I got through it. And I graduated.

## Where the Fog Finally Cleared

It wasn't until I started working as a full-stack developer that the fog actually cleared.

Math, it turned out, was everywhere. Just not in the form I'd been trained to expect. It showed up whenever I had to take a messy, complicated problem and turn it into something simple enough to actually solve. The logic I reached for to work through problems at the office, day after day, wasn't separate from math. It was math — just without a whiteboard, a formula sheet, or a teacher asking me to show my work.

## What I'd Tell My Younger Self

So if I could go back and answer my friend from that linear equations class, I'd tell him this: no, you probably won't solve for x by hand ever again. But the way of thinking that problem was trying to teach you? You'll use it every single day. You just won't recognize it as math until years later.
