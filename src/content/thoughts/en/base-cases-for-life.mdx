Every recursive function needs a base case, or it never stops.

Human reasoning has the same requirement, it turns out. When a problem is too tangled to hold in your head, you don't solve the tangled version. You manufacture a smaller, solvable one, solve that, and carry the answer back up. Richard Zeckhauser, a Harvard Kennedy School professor, built two of his "maxims for thinking analytically" entirely around this move.

## Go to an Extreme Case

Push one variable in the problem to its extreme (zero, infinity, "what if nothing happens at all") and the right intuition, or the wrong assumption, usually falls out immediately.

The classic version: Mary can paint a room alone in 2 hours, Jim alone in 3. How long together? Most people average the two and blurt out 2.5 hours.

Wrong.

Push Jim's contribution to the extreme: imagine he does nothing, just watches. The answer is still capped at 2 hours, however long Mary takes alone. So if Jim is actually helping at all, the real answer has to be less than 2 hours. Not more than the average.

This isn't just a math trick. Zeckhauser finds the same move everywhere once he goes looking.

In negotiation: push a "proportional" profit split to an extreme ratio like 2000:1, and its unfairness becomes obvious instantly, obvious in a way it wasn't at a reasonable-looking split.

In policy design: the US Department of Labor stress-tested an education subsidy by imagining it had no restrictions at all on how it could be used.

In business: run the worst-case scenario first. Survive the worst stress test and everything milder is already handled.

## Go to a Simple Case

The sibling move. Instead of pushing a variable to its extreme, strip the whole problem down to the fewest moving parts possible, find the insight there, then generalize back to the messier real version.

Zeckhauser once proudly told his own mentor, Howard Raiffa, that he'd simplified a group-decision problem down to three types of agents. Raiffa's reply: "now try it with two."

A student paralyzed for years over choosing between an Economics PhD and a Public Policy PhD got unstuck with one simplifying question: do you want to answer a narrow question precisely, or a big question imprecisely?

There's a "Rule of Three" version too. A youth baseball coach's detailed technical breakdown of batting mechanics put 11-year-olds to sleep. It worked once he cut it down to three words: "Hands, Hips, Head."

Worth saying clearly: simplifying isn't the coward's way out. Choosing the right technique intelligently beats virtuoso computation aimed in the wrong direction, every time.

## Why This Is Recursion, Not Just a Trick

A recursive function without a base case is a stack overflow waiting to happen.

These two maxims are how you manufacture a base case for a real-world problem that doesn't come with one built in. Same instinct as binary search cutting a big, fuzzy search space down to a single well-defined comparison. Same instinct as `factorial(0)` giving recursion somewhere to bottom out.

They're complementary, not competing. "Simple case" reduces the number of moving parts. "Extreme case" reduces a single variable to its boundary. Zeckhauser pairs them himself — simplify first, then push what's left to its extreme.

And once the reduced version is solved, the insight doesn't stay down there. You carry it back up the call stack, the same way a recursive call's return value climbs back to the original invocation.

## Personal Reflection

I already do this. Code review arguments that get too abstract to reason about, I default to: what if there's only one item in the list. What if this runs zero times. What if two requests hit this at the exact same millisecond.

That's maxim 1 and maxim 2 running as debugging heuristics, not math.

The painting-room mistake is the same shape of mistake I see in estimation and capacity planning constantly: averaging instead of reasoning from the extreme. The average feels safe. It's often quietly wrong.
