## Short Explanation

Mawar Florist is a flower shop in Yogyakarta. Before this project they took every order over WhatsApp, and there was no real way for customers to pay online or for the owner to see what was actually selling. I built them a web app to replace that.

It has two sides. Customers get a storefront to browse flowers, add things to a cart, and pay. The owner gets a dashboard to manage products, orders, discounts, and customers. Payments run through a gateway that confirms the order and generates a PDF invoice on its own, so nobody has to sit and reply to messages all day just to close a sale.

## Project Goals

The main thing the owner wanted was to stop running the shop out of a chat inbox. Customers should be able to order and pay by themselves, and there should be one place to see everything instead of scrolling back through messages.

So the platform needed to:

- Let people browse products and check out, whether or not they have an account
- Accept the payment methods Indonesians actually use — QRIS, e-wallets, and bank transfer
- Confirm paid orders and issue invoices without anyone doing it by hand
- Give the owner a dashboard for products, orders, discounts, and customers
- Handle Indonesian addresses properly, down to the village

Basically, let the owner deal with flowers instead of admin work, and let a customer finish a purchase without ever needing to message anyone.

## Features

### 1. Customer Storefront

A public catalog with search, category filters, and product detail pages with image galleries. Customers can build a cart as a guest or while logged in, and complete checkout with a saved shipping address.

### 2. Online Payment Checkout

Checkout uses Xendit's hosted invoice flow. The customer picks QRIS, an e-wallet, or bank transfer, pays on Xendit's page, and comes back to the site. No payment details ever pass through my app, which keeps a whole category of security headaches off my plate.

### 3. Automated Payment Confirmation

When a payment goes through, Xendit sends a webhook. The app checks the token, matches it to the right order, and flips the status to confirmed. The owner doesn't have to check anything or confirm anything manually.

### 4. Automatic Invoice Generation

As soon as an order is confirmed, a PDF invoice gets generated and saved for the customer to download. It's built to be idempotent, so if Xendit fires the same webhook twice (which happens), the customer still ends up with one invoice, not two.

### 5. Admin Dashboard

A KPI dashboard plus full management for products, orders, discounts, customers, transactions, and reports. Includes multi-image product upload, order status timelines, discount codes with usage limits, and CSV customer export.

### 6. Indonesian Address System

Cascading Province → Regency → District → Village dropdowns backed by the full Indonesian region dataset. It's a small thing, but it means a delivery address is actually usable instead of someone typing a village name three different ways.

## The Problems and How I Addressed It

The part that worried me most was the payment webhook. That's how the gateway tells you an order is paid, but the endpoint is public, so in theory anyone could send a fake "paid" event and get free flowers. I handled it by checking Xendit's callback token on every request, looking the order up in my own database instead of trusting whatever came in the payload, and only treating specific gateway statuses as "paid." On top of that I made the invoice step idempotent, mostly because Xendit sometimes sends the same webhook more than once and I didn't want duplicate invoices piling up.

The other thing I had to think about was old orders. What happens to an order from last month if the owner later renames a product or deletes it? I didn't want history to quietly change or break, so I store a copy of the product title and price on each order item at the time of purchase, and I use soft deletes for products and orders instead of really removing them. An old order keeps showing what the customer actually bought.

Halfway through the project the shop switched payment providers, from Midtrans to Xendit. That could have been a mess, but I'd kept all the payment code in one service class instead of sprinkling it through the controllers, so the switch mostly came down to rewriting that one file and running a migration. The checkout flow itself barely moved.

## Lessons Learned

The thing that stuck with me is that most of the hard work on an online store isn't the shopping cart — it's the boring, defensive stuff around money. Making sure a payment can't be faked and an invoice can't be duplicated took more thought than the whole product catalog did, and it's the kind of thing you only notice when it goes wrong.

Keeping the payment logic in one place also turned out to be worth it. At the start it felt like an extra layer I didn't strictly need, but when the shop changed providers I only had to touch one service. I'll be doing that with any third-party integration from now on, since you rarely stick with the first one.

And building both the storefront and the admin side made me realize how easy it is to call a feature "done" too early. It's not enough that a customer can place an order — the owner also has to be able to find it, update it, and fix it when something's off. I'd have missed half of that if I'd only built the customer-facing part.
